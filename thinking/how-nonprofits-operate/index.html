    For developers, QA, PMs, sales, internsIndia contextv1.0 · Sept 2026
    The one question this guide prepares you to ask

    "Show me how the people move, how the money moves, and how the evidence moves."

    Ask it on your next discovery call. By the end of this guide you'll understand the answer.

    Why this existsWe build for work most of us have never done

    A developer who has never seen a utilisation certificate will build a budget module that looks right and fails the first audit. A PM who doesn't know why a donor ID matters on a beneficiary record will cut it as "extra scope".

    None of that is a skills problem. It's a context problem. This guide gives you the context: who's in the room, where the money comes from and what it's allowed to do, how a person moves through a programme, how anyone knows it worked, and where technology fits in all of it.

    Running this as a team programme? schedules and facilitation

    Weekly (recommended): one chapter a week for five weeks, then a capstone week. 30 minutes of reading before each session, 60 minutes of homework after, always on the same NGO.

    2-day bootcamp (new joiners): Day 1 covers chapters 1, 2 and a compressed 3. Day 2 covers 4, 5 and a mini capstone at 20 minutes per team.

    Principles that make it work:

    • Use real artefacts, anonymised. A redacted FUR template teaches more than a slide about FURs. Strip every name, phone number, Aadhaar and amount that could identify a person.
    • 70/30: learners talk 70% of the time.
    • Always end on "so what for EdZola?" Every concept should land as a field, a workflow, a validation, a dashboard or a discovery question.
    • Open each session with the myth vs reality cards. Vote first, reveal later.
    • Mix roles. Put a developer with a salesperson. One learns why the requirement exists, the other learns what it costs to build.

    1 · The big ideaA nonprofit is a business with two customers

    A company has one customer who both pays and receives the product. A nonprofit splits that customer in two. The person who pays (a CSR department, a foundation, a donor) is not the person who receives the service (a student, a patient, a farmer).

    Funderpays
    Nonprofitdelivers
    Beneficiaryreceives
    Funder → Nonprofitmoney and expectations
    Nonprofit → Beneficiaryservices
    Nonprofit → Funderreports and evidence
    Beneficiary → Nonprofitfeedback, often weak or missing

    So the nonprofit has to prove to one party what happened to another. That single fact explains most of what we build:

    • Reporting is heavy because the funder can't see the service directly.
    • Beneficiary IDs matter because you need to prove the same person was served, not counted twice.
    • Feedback loops are weak because the beneficiary doesn't pay, so their voice has no built-in channel.
    • Donor fields show up in programme forms because the money has to be traced to the person.
    In a company, the customer tells you the product is bad by not buying it. In a nonprofit, the beneficiary usually can't. Our systems are part of how their voice gets heard.

    4 · Operating modelsHow the work actually gets done

    Direct delivery

    Own staff serve people

    Needs deep beneficiary tracking, attendance, staff performance, field mobile apps.

    Teach For India places fellows in classrooms. Magic Bus runs sport-based life skills and livelihoods through its own staff.
    Partner-led / grantmaking

    Fund and support other NGOs

    Needs partner onboarding, due diligence, grant tracking, partner reports, portfolio roll-ups.

    Azim Premji Foundation and Tata Trusts fund and support large partner bases.
    Government partnership

    Strengthen public systems

    Needs government formats, district and block hierarchies, huge scale, light data entry per school.

    Pratham's Teaching at the Right Level runs inside government schools. Akshaya Patra delivers PM POSHAN mid-day meals with states.
    Network / intermediary

    Convene, research, advocate

    Needs relationship CRM, knowledge management, event and cohort tracking.

    Dasra (philanthropy and research), GuideStar India (credibility), Sattva (advisory).
    Hybrid / earned revenue

    Earn and subsidise

    Needs revenue accounting and impact tracking, cost per unit, subsidy ratios.

    Aravind Eye Care: paying patients cross-subsidise free surgeries. Goonj: urban surplus becomes "Cloth for Work" in villages.

    Most real organisations mix models. Akshaya Patra runs its own central kitchens (direct delivery), inside a government scheme (government partnership), funded partly by government cost norms and partly by donors. That mix shapes every requirement.

    The classic mistake: one Excel sheet per donor, with the same child appearing in three sheets because three donors each fund part of the same school. The fix is one beneficiary record linked to many projects. The project link is what lets you answer each donor honestly without double-counting.

    Chapter 2 · Follow the money · about 45 minutes

    Follow the ₹1 crore

    Where money comes from, what it's allowed to be spent on, and how an NGO proves what happened to it. The heaviest chapter, and the one that explains the most requirements.

    By the end you can

    • Walk the 12-stage money lifecycle from donor to renewal without notes
    • Tell apart the eight money jobs people lump together as "fundraising" or "finance"
    • Explain restricted vs unrestricted money, hard vs soft commitments, and tranches
    • Name the compliance rules that shape a design: FCRA, 80G, 10BD/10BE, CSR-1, 12A/12AB
    The donor's question

    "We gave you ₹1 crore. What happened to it, and what did it achieve?"

    Think of an NGO you know. Could they answer this in a day? In a week? Keep that answer in mind as you read.

    1 · The lifecycleTwelve stages, one loop

    Tap through the stages, or press play. Then say it out loud until it's automatic: donor, proposal, grant, agreement, budget, allocation, spend, utilisation, evidence, report, audit, renewal.

    Renewal loops back to the funder. The relationship continues.

    The last arrow matters most. A late, messy or unconvincing report doesn't just annoy the donor. It kills the renewal, and renewals are the cheapest money an NGO ever raises.

    Rules that shape the design

    • Budgets are multi-dimensional: programme × geography × activity × HR/non-HR × FCRA/non-FCRA × financial year.
    • Activity codes must sum to the project budget. Validate it.
    • Cost centres usually start in accounting (Tally or Zoho Books) and sync into the programme or grant system. Sync direction and frequency is a design decision, not a detail.
    • Shared costs get apportioned. A programme manager on three projects at 40/35/25 is split across three grants. No timesheets? Then a quarterly staff-project matrix.
    • Admin is capped. Donors often cap indirect costs at 5 to 15%. FCRA caps administrative expenses at 20% of foreign contributions received in the year.

    Where it breaks

    • A training request goes through without checking the remaining budget line, and the PM quietly moves it to another head. That's the request ↔ budget seam.
    • Expenses are booked in Tally without a project code and reconciled by hand at year-end.
    • FCRA and domestic money are mixed at the budget level, which makes FC-4 painful.

    Chapter 3 · Follow the person · about 35 minutes

    From first contact to real change

    How a person moves through a programme, where they drop off, and why "one person, one record" is the most important rule in programme data.

    By the end you can

    • Draw the generic 7-stage journey and adapt it to education, skilling and health
    • Tell outputs from outcomes, and explain why outcomes need a follow-up
    • Spot drop-off points and design a status model with reasons
    • Handle consent and personal data the way the DPDP Act and EdZola policy require
    A story firstPriya's journey

    Priya is 19 and lives two hours from the nearest town. A mobiliser meets her at a community meeting and signs her up for a retail skilling batch. Week three, her attendance drops: her mother is ill and she's needed at home. The centre calls, shifts her to an afternoon slot, and she comes back.

    She completes the course and gets placed at a store in town. Two months in, the commute is eating her salary and she's about to quit. A follow-up call catches it, and the placement team helps her find a shared room near the store. Six months later she's still employed.

    Twice Priya almost became a dropout statistic. Both times, what saved her was someone knowing, in time, that something had changed. Keep her in mind for the rest of this chapter.

    The shape of a real funnel

    An illustrative skilling programme. The red bars are the stages with the steepest drop from the one before.

    Each gap is a question a programme manager should be able to answer, and one our system should help with: who dropped, where, why, and what did we do about it?

    Donors fund the top of this funnel. They judge you on the bottom. The system lives in between.

    2 · Outputs vs outcomesThe most important distinction in the sector

    Inputs₹, staff, material
    Activitieswhat we do
    Outputswhat we delivered: countable, immediate
    Outcomeswhat changed for the person: needs follow-up
    Impactlong-term change: needs evaluation
    SectorOutputOutcome
    Education1,200 students attended 40 sessions610 students moved up a learning level
    Skilling830 youth completed training420 still employed 6 months later
    Health5,000 women screened for anaemia3,100 moved from moderate to mild or normal Hb
    Livelihoods800 kits distributed for community workA village built a 2 km road, used by 12 hamlets
    • Outputs come from transactional data (attendance, deliveries) and can be counted automatically.
    • Outcomes need a later event: a follow-up call, an endline assessment, a placement check. No follow-up entity in the data model means no outcomes, only outputs dressed up.
    • Most "we need a dashboard" requests really mean "we can't count outcomes because nobody records follow-up".

    4 · Five rulesWhat makes journey data work

    Rule 1: One person, one record

    Wrong
    Sheet "CSR Co A 2025":  Priya S, 14
Sheet "Foundation B":   Priya Sharma, 14
Sheet "Baseline":       P. Sharma, age 13

→ counted 3 times, with 3 ages
    Right
    BEN-00417  (one record)
 ├── Project: CSR Co A FY25
 ├── Project: Foundation B FY25-27
 ├── Assessment: Baseline
 └── Attendance: 38 sessions

    Use a stable unique ID, duplicate checks (name + date of birth + guardian + village), and relationships instead of copies.

    Rule 2: Status is a journey, not a checkbox

    • Never just Active/Inactive when there are more real states.
    • Every terminal state carries a reason from a master list: migrated, married, got a job, lost interest, family objection.
    • Status changes are dated, so you see when people left, not just that they left.

    Rule 3: Every data point earns its place

    Name the decision each field serves and who looks at it. Every field is a tax on a field worker standing in the sun with a phone at 12% battery.

    Rule 4: Data flows back

    If the field officer who enters attendance never sees her own batch's attendance trend, the system is extractive. Adoption drops, data quality drops, and then the donor report is wrong. Design the field officer's view first.

    Rule 5: Design for the real field

    • Offline-first mobile forms that sync later
    • Local language labels
    • Photo and geo-tag where donors require it, not everywhere
    • Fewer free-text fields, more dropdowns from master tables
    Try thisFeel the field tax10 min · with a colleague
    1. Find a real intake form from a past project (redacted) or any long online registration form.
    2. One of you plays a field worker on a slow phone. The other reads every field aloud, one at a time, and waits for an answer.
    3. Time it. Then cut the form in half. For every field you keep, name the decision it serves.
    What usually happensSomewhere around field 20, the "field worker" starts guessing. That's where junk data comes from.

    5 · Consent and dignityNot optional, and not just a legal box

    PrincipleWhat it means in practice
    Consent (DPDP Act 2023)Record that consent was given, for what purpose, when, and by whom (a guardian for minors). A consent field, date and version on the beneficiary record
    Purpose limitationCollect only what the programme needs. "We might need caste later" is not a purpose
    MinimisationDon't store Aadhaar unless a scheme requires it. If you must, mask it and restrict access. Never plain text
    Access controlField staff see their beneficiaries; donors see aggregates, never names
    Dignity in stories and photosSeparate consent for photos and case stories used in donor reports
    EdZola policyNon-negotiable

    Never paste real beneficiary names, phone numbers, Aadhaar, health data or donor records into Claude or any AI tool. For training, use anonymised or synthetic data only.

    1 · Why measureThree audiences, three purposes

    Accountability

    "Did we do what we promised?"

    For funders, the board and regulators.

    Learning

    "What's working, what isn't?"

    For programme teams.

    Management

    "Who needs help this week?"

    For day-to-day decisions.

    Most NGO systems are built only for the first one. That's why they feel like compliance, and why field teams resent them. A good system serves all three, and management use is what keeps the data honest, because people fix data they rely on.

    If data only flows up, people treat it as compliance. If it flows back, they treat it as a tool.

    2 · ToC, logframe, indicatorsThe story, the contract and the data model

    Bonus read · for anyone coming from the corporate world · about 20 minutes

    Why isn't it all digital yet?

    If you've come from a company, a startup or consulting, you'll probably look at a nonprofit and think: I could fix this in a month. Here's why that instinct usually backfires, and what to do with it instead.

    By the end you can

    • Name the structural constraints that make nonprofit technology different
    • Spot which corporate playbooks transfer, which need adapting, and which break
    • Explain to a new colleague why "boiling the ocean" fails, and what to do in week one instead
    ReflectBe honest with yourself first

    Before you read this, write down what you assumed about nonprofits when you joined. Why do you think they aren't more digitised? What would you have done in your first month?

    The instinct"Why don't they just…"

    Every new joiner from another sector says some version of this in their first week:

    Heard often

    "Why don't they just buy Salesforce?"

    Heard often

    "Let's standardise the process across all 40 centres."

    Heard often

    "We'll roll it out to everyone on 1 April."

    Heard often

    "Just get them to hire an IT person."

    The instinct is well meant, and part of it is right: most nonprofits genuinely could use their data far better. What's wrong is the diagnosis. Nonprofits aren't behind because people there are less capable or less willing. They're behind because the conditions they work in are different, and most technology (and most consultants) are designed for conditions they don't have.

    Nonprofits don't lack software. They lack the money, time, people and process clarity that make software stick.

    The constraintsTen things that are structurally different

    Tap each constraint to see what it means in practice and what to do about it.

    Side by sideCorporate assumption vs nonprofit reality

    Corporate assumptionNonprofit realityWhat works instead
    There's a tech budgetTech is overhead. Overhead is capped, often at 5 to 15% of a grant, and rarely covers systemsPhase the work so each phase fits one grant or one funder's capacity-building line
    Revenue funds the next releaseMost money is restricted to programmes and runs 1 to 3 yearsDesign for a minimum that survives without us; template, don't custom-build
    The customer defines requirementsThe funder often does, and every funder wants a different reportModel the data once; generate each donor's format from it
    Standardise the processEach geography, partner and donor contract carries real variationStandardise the data model and definitions; allow variation in workflow
    Big-bang launchField teams can't stop delivering to learn a new systemPilot one workflow in one centre; roll out between programme peaks
    Users adopt because management says soField staff have been handed "the new system" before, and watched it dieCo-design with field staff; give them their own numbers back from week one
    Hire an IT teamThere's one "accidental techie" who also runs a programmeBuild for that person: simple admin, documentation, handholding
    Fail fast, iterate in productionMistakes land on vulnerable people: a lost scholarship, a missed referralTest with synthetic data; keep a human decision in every high-stakes step
    Measure ROI in monthsOutcomes take years, and the payer isn't the person who benefitsMeasure adoption and time saved early; outcomes later
    Data is an asset to exploitBeneficiary data is held in trust for people with little power to objectCollect less; consent, mask and restrict by default

    Boiling the oceanWhy trying to fix everything at once fails

    A typical "transform everything" plan looks like this: new CRM, new MIS, new HRMS, new dashboards and an AI layer, all in one financial year. Here's what usually happens next.

    Month 1Big kickoff. Every team lists every requirement.
    Month 3Scope doubles. Requirements conflict across teams.
    Month 5March arrives. Everyone disappears into utilisation reports.
    Month 7Go-live with half the modules. Training is one session.
    Month 10Staff are back on Excel, running both in parallel.

    The failure isn't technical. The organisation simply doesn't have the spare capacity to absorb that much change at once.

    One pot at a time

    The alternative is less exciting and far more likely to work.

    01
    One decisionPick the single decision that hurts most (for example "which batches are losing people?"). Name who makes it and when.
    02
    One workflowMap the smallest workflow that feeds that decision. Agree the definitions.
    03
    One teamPilot it with one centre or one team. Give them their own numbers back.
    04
    One seasonRun it through a full cycle, including a reporting crunch. Fix what breaks.
    05
    Then widenRoll out, add the next workflow, and only then think about AI.

    What transfersYour corporate experience isn't wasted

    Plenty of what you know is exactly what nonprofits need. It just has to be applied at the right size and pace.

    Bring it

    • Clear definitions and a single source of truth
    • Process mapping and swimlanes
    • Project discipline: scope, sprints, UAT, sign-off
    • Dashboards built around decisions
    • Security and access control habits

    Adapt it

    • ROI → time saved per field worker, reports that go out on time
    • Standardisation → shared data model, flexible workflows
    • Change management → co-design, handholding, 30 to 45 days of hyper-care
    • Roadmaps → phases that fit funding cycles and the FY calendar
    • "Users" → people with 12% battery, patchy signal and a full day of fieldwork

    Your first weekFive habits for people new to the sector

    1. Visit the field before you design anything. Sit with a field officer for a day. Watch where the data actually gets written down.
    2. Learn their words. Participant, learner, member, child. Use theirs, not yours.
    3. Ask about the last system. "What did you try before, and what happened?" The answer tells you what they're afraid of.
    4. Find the calendar. When are reports due? When does the CSR money arrive? Plan around it.
    5. Assume competence. The workaround that looks messy usually exists for a reason. Find the reason before you remove it.
