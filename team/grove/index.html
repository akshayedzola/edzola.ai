    What we believe about the sectorSix beliefs, in plain words

    You'll hear these lines in proposals, on calls and in reviews. Open each one to see what it actually means, what skill it asks of you, and what it isn't.

    1. Mission-rich, systems-poorNonprofits have deep purpose and know-how. What they lack is time, tools and process.
    In plain words
    The people we work with are experts in their communities. They usually don't lack commitment or ideas. They lack the time, money and support to build good systems, so they run on spreadsheets, paper and memory.
    What it asks of you
    Respect before redesign. Learn how they work today before proposing anything. Ask for a walkthrough of their current system, even if it's 40 Excel tabs. That sheet is years of hard-won logic.
    Example
    Staff who resist a new system are often being rational: the last system was designed without them. Involve them early and resistance turns into ownership.
    Not this
    "They don't understand tech, we'll tell them what they need."
    2. Technology is not neutralEvery design choice decides who gets counted and who gets left out.
    In plain words
    A system isn't just a container for data. The fields you make mandatory, the language you use, the device you assume, all decide who can take part and what gets seen.
    What it asks of you
    For every design decision, ask: "Who does this leave out?" Then design for the hardest user, not the easiest.
    Example
    A mandatory phone number field excludes a woman who shares her husband's phone. An English-only form excludes the field worker. A dashboard that counts "sessions held" hides whether anyone learned anything.
    Not this
    "It's just a form."
    3. Expand what's possible, don't just automateCopying paper onto a screen saves a little time. Rethinking the work changes outcomes.
    In plain words
    Digitising a process exactly as it is gives you the same process, a bit faster. The bigger win is asking what the organisation could do now that it couldn't before.
    What it asks of you
    Process thinking. Map the current workflow, find the steps nobody needs, and ask "what decision would this data help someone make?"
    Example
    Instead of a digital copy of the attendance register, flag any student absent three days in a row so a counsellor calls home that evening.
    Not this
    A pixel-perfect replica of the paper register, including the columns nobody reads.
    4. Adoption is social, not technicalA system nobody uses is a failed system, however good the code.
    In plain words
    People use systems they trust and helped shape. Training, champions, follow-up sessions and patience are part of the build, not extras.
    What it asks of you
    Change management. Find a champion at the partner, show early versions, train in their language, and come back after go-live.
    Example
    We treat handover as a phase: three follow-up sessions over six weeks, and weekly usage reports so we can see adoption, not assume it. "People own what they help build. They comply with what they're told."
    Not this
    Go-live equals done.
    5. Fitness over ideologyWe pick the tool that fits the partner, not the tool we like.
    In plain words
    Zoho is our main platform and we're very good at it. But the right answer for a small organisation might be Google Sheets, KoboToolbox, or something else entirely.
    What it asks of you
    Honest judgement about size, skills, budget and future. Recommend the smallest thing that solves the real problem.
    Example
    "Staying loyal to a vendor at the expense of a partner's actual problem is a quiet form of dishonesty." A free tool nobody knows how to use isn't free either.
    Not this
    Every problem looks like a Creator app.
    6. Sector translator, not software vendorWe speak programme and we speak tech, and we translate both ways.
    In plain words
    A vendor builds what's in the requirement. A translator understands the programme (beneficiaries, outcomes, donors, FCRA) and the technology (entities, workflows, APIs), and helps each side understand the other.
    What it asks of you
    Learn how nonprofits operate. Explain tech in the partner's words. Tell partners clearly what we can't do well.
    Example
    "We have not ventured into mobile apps yet, but I'd love to listen." Saying what we can't do has won us more trust than overpromising ever did.
    Not this
    Sending a requirements document full of jargon and calling it alignment.

    Two lines you'll hear a lot

    "Every data point must earn its place"Collect less, use all of it.

    Every field in a form has a cost: someone's time to fill it, someone's time to check it, and a risk if it's personal data. Before adding a field, ask who will use it to decide what. If there's no answer, leave it out.

    "Thinking → Artifact. Always."Every conversation ends in something you can see.

    A good discovery call ends with a map, a prototype or a one-page summary, not just notes. A good idea ends with a draft. It's how we make thinking visible and how partners feel progress early. With Claude, the distance between an idea and an artifact is now minutes.

    Stop 3 of 9 · Weathering

    When the storms come

    Some storms are inside you: stuck, unclear, overwhelmed. Some come from partners, some from the sector, some from EdZola itself. Storms are part of the climate here. What matters is how we stand in them, together.

    15 min readA storm guideA sorting game5 quiz questions

    Four teachers for bad weatherResilience looks ordinary

    The coat-buttons weed

    Shows up anyway

    Grows in the crack by the bus stop. Dust, horns, heat, nobody watering it. It doesn't need the day to go well to show up.

    The coconut palm

    Bends, doesn't snap

    In a coastal cyclone the palm sways almost flat and stands up again. Flexibility is strength. Plans change; you adjust and keep going.

    The mangrove

    Roots that hold together

    Dense mangroves in the Sundarbans can cut cyclone wave height by around 60%. Not one tree: a tangle of roots holding each other. That's a team in a storm.

    The lotus

    Grows out of the mud

    The lotus roots in murky water and still opens clean every morning. Our work happens in messy contexts. We don't wait for clean conditions.

    Resilience here isn't toughing it out alone. It's showing up steadily, asking for help early, and not taking every storm personally. A partner changing their mind is weather, not a verdict on you.

    Field note

    "The expectation is not perfection. The expectation is deliberate practice, reflection, and visible improvement over time."

    Akshay, June 2026

    The storm guideWhat are you facing right now?

    Pick the storm closest to what you're feeling or facing. Each one has a first move for today, who to bring in, what to avoid, and how EdZola thinks about it.

    The weather scaleNot every mistake is the same weather

    We respond to mistakes in proportion. Most are drizzle. The first two are how we learn. The last two are rare, and serious.

    Drizzle: a new mistakeWe learn from it. Write down what happened, fix it, move on.
    Rain: it happened because something was unclearWe fix the system: the spec, the checklist, the template. Systems fail, not people.
    Wind: the same mistake again, after feedbackA capability conversation with your lead. What's missing: clarity, skill, or something else?
    Storm: careless and high-impactA clear consequence, proportionate to what happened.
    Cyclone: an integrity or boundary breachSerious action, whoever you are. Think false status, hidden risk, or leaking partner data.
    Try itWhat kind of weather is this?

    Your storm kitThree habits that keep you standing

    1. The stuck timer

    Stuck for 30 to 45 minutes? Ask for help, with what you've tried. Two days stuck without telling anyone is not OK. It isn't brave, it just moves the storm onto your partner.

    2. The help ladder

    Ask a peer at your level first. Then someone a level up. Then your lead. The founder is the last stop, not the first. Every step you climb, bring what you've tried. Claude is a great first rung for thinking out loud, but it doesn't replace telling a human you're stuck.

    3. The blocker protocol

    1. Name it the day it appears. Not at the weekly call. Today.
    2. State the impact specifically. "UAT slips four days" beats "there's some delay".
    3. Name the unblocker. Who can actually move this, and by when?
    4. Bring the partner in if it's on their side. Early and kindly.
    5. Write it down in Zoho Projects or the project channel. Memory is not a system.
    "Unless people start speaking up, no one is going to help them magically from the sky."Akshay, delivery governance call, 2026

    CraftSix working instincts, explained

    These came out of six years of projects, good and bad. Open each one.

    1. Clarity before codeKnow what done looks like before you build anything.
    What it means
    Before writing a line of Deluge or a prompt, you can explain in plain words what the partner needs, why, and how you'll know it works.
    Why
    Most rework at EdZola traces back to requirements nobody wrote down. "A fast wrong build becomes a slow expensive rebuild."
    Looks like
    A written summary within 24 hours of a requirement call. At least three acceptance criteria per story. A design review before sprint one.
    Not this
    "Let's start building and we'll figure it out."
    2. Specificity before commitmentPromise things that can be checked.
    What it means
    Every commitment has a what, a when and a who. Every scope change has a price.
    Why
    Vague promises create silent disappointment. Specific ones can be planned, tracked and renegotiated honestly.
    Looks like
    "Filters and export on the attendance report, by Thursday 5 PM, shared with @QA." "That change is two extra days. Should we swap it for the PDF export?"
    Not this
    "Soon." "Almost done." Discounting by reflex.
    3. Make work visible, earlyShare at 60%, not 100%.
    What it means
    Show rough work while it can still change: a sketch, a half-built screen, a draft doc.
    Why
    "A prototype that reveals a mismatch in week two is a gift. The same mismatch discovered in month five is a disaster."
    Looks like
    One working prototype by Friday. Internal demos before client demos. Daily Priorities that show real progress.
    Not this
    Disappearing for two weeks and returning with something polished and wrong.
    4. Documentation before memoryIf it's only in your head, it leaves when you do.
    What it means
    Decisions, logic, metric definitions and handovers get written down where the team can find them.
    Why
    We once built a dashboard where "every number was correct. Nobody used it", because the one person who could explain the metrics had left.
    Looks like
    Clear field names, a README for every module, decision notes in the project channel, a KT handover before anyone leaves.
    Not this
    "Ask the person who built it. Oh, they left."
    5. Parallel momentumBlocked on one thing? Move another.
    What it means
    When you're waiting on a partner or a teammate, keep the pieces that don't depend on them moving.
    Why
    Waiting is the biggest hidden cost in our projects. Partners are busy; decisions take time.
    Looks like
    Waiting on sign-off for the form? Build the reports on sample data. Waiting on data? Write the test cases.
    Not this
    "Blocked" as a status for a week.
    6. Recovery before blameFix it for the partner first. Find the root cause after.
    What it means
    When something breaks, the first job is to restore the partner's work. The second is a calm root cause analysis. Blame is never a step.
    Why
    Systems fail, not people. People who fear blame hide problems, and hidden problems become cyclones.
    Looks like
    A quick fix and a note to the partner, then a 5 Whys RCA, then a change to the checklist so it never repeats.
    Not this
    "Who did this?" as the first question.

    Quality, every time

    • QA on every task, from requirements onward. Developers document unit tests before handing over.
    • Only QA moves a task from "dev testing" to "to be tested". QA can reject an incomplete handover.
    • An internal demo happens before every client demo.
    • Every bug gets a root cause analysis with the 5 Whys, so it never repeats. There's a Claude skill for it.
    • Closing a project means seven things: milestones and timesheets closed, a case study, technical docs, a user manual, invoicing sign-off, a retrospective, and a plan for what's next with the partner.

    Growing AI-native

    Moso bamboo can grow over a metre in a single day. It can only do that because of the root network it spent years building underground. AI gives us that speed. Software fundamentals, partner context and judgement are the roots.

    30 min readA learning mapTwo sorting games7 quiz questions

    Why this mattersThe world is going AI-native. So are we.

    "If technology changes before EdZola does, then EdZola is at risk."Akshay, delivery governance call, Sep 2026

    Every full-time EdZolan has Claude on our Team plan. Using it daily is part of the job. Claude is not optional. The goal we've set: at least 30% of our daily delivery work done with Claude's help. The test: if something takes two days, see if you can do it in two hours.

    The threshold to become a builder has fallen. English is now a programming language. Whatever your role, you'll be building here. But fast growth on weak roots falls over in the first monsoon. So this stop starts underground.

    Roots, part 1What makes good software

    AI can write code. It can't tell you whether you're building the right thing, whether your data model will survive next year, or whether a field worker can actually use the screen. That's the craft. Here are the six roots every EdZolan should grow, technical or not.

    Root 1 · Problem first, tool lastProduct thinking: understand the job before choosing the tool.

    "Tools feel like progress. Problems feel like delay." Before any build, answer three questions: what's happening today that shouldn't be? What does success look like, in numbers? Is this actually a software problem, or a process or people problem?

    Jobs to be done: people "hire" a system to get a job done. A programme manager doesn't want a dashboard; she wants to walk into the donor meeting confident. Design for the job.

    Start small: build the smallest thing that solves the core problem and test it with real users. Think skateboard first, then bicycle, then car, not one wheel at a time. Fix the time you'll spend and let the scope flex, not the other way round.

    Root 2 · Data modellingGet the entities and relationships right, and everything else gets easier.

    A data model is a map of the things an organisation cares about and how they connect. Get it wrong and every report becomes a workaround. Get it right and the system bends instead of breaking when the programme changes.

    • Entities are the things: Student, Batch, Session, Attendance, Donor, Donation, Grant.
    • Attributes are facts about them: a student's date of birth, a session's date.
    • Relationships connect them. One batch has many students (one-to-many). Students attend many sessions, and sessions have many students (many-to-many, so you need a linking record: Attendance).
    • Keys: every record has a unique ID (primary key). Other records point to it (foreign key). Never identify a person by name alone; two Priyas will break your reports.
    • Master data and lookups: districts, schools and programme types live in one master table, picked from a list, never typed freely. "Coimbatore", "CBE" and "coimbatore " are three districts to a computer.

    Normalisation, in plain words: every fact lives in exactly one place. One value per cell (first normal form). Facts about the student live in the student record, not copied into every attendance row (second and third normal forms). Then a name change happens once, not 400 times. Try the demo below.

    ImpactOS gives us canonical entities for nonprofits so we don't start from scratch every time. Use the ImpactOS Entity Library skill.

    Root 3 · Process mapping and workflowMap how work really flows before you automate it.

    "Automate a confused workflow and you get a confused workflow at scale." Before building, map the process:

    • As-is, then to-be. Draw how it works today (the real way, not the procedure manual), then how it should work.
    • Swimlanes. One lane per role: field worker, coordinator, programme manager, finance. Problems hide in the handoffs between lanes.
    • Don't automate ambiguity. If two people do a step differently, agree the right way first.
    • Who does what is who can do what. A RACI (who's responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) is almost exactly a permissions matrix for the system.

    We once built a system from a procedure manual that was five years out of date. It was "elegant and completely useless". Walk the real process.

    Root 4 · Design for the person using itUX for the field: fewer fields, clearer words, works on a cheap phone.
    • Design for the hardest user: a low-end Android phone, patchy network, maybe not English, maybe low literacy.
    • Fewer fields. Every data point must earn its place. Use defaults and dropdowns over free text.
    • Clear words. "Build for the next person": label a field "Application Review Status (Pending / Approved / Rejected)", not "Status".
    • Prevent errors instead of explaining them: validate at entry, show what went wrong and how to fix it.
    • Consistency. Same action, same place, every screen. Use Nielsen's ten usability heuristics as a checklist for every screen you review.
    Root 5 · Design principles for software that lastsSimple, reusable, testable, safe.
    • Keep it simple. The simplest thing that works is easiest to maintain, explain and hand over. Build less, better.
    • Don't repeat yourself. If you've built it twice, make it a function, template or skill. That's how we stop rebuilding the same system.
    • You aren't going to need it. Don't build for imagined future requirements. Build for this one, cleanly.
    • Separate data, logic and screens. A change to a report shouldn't break the form.
    • Configure, don't hardcode. Three webinar types today will be five next year. Put them in a master table.
    • Test beyond the happy path. Empty values, duplicates, wrong formats, slow network, bulk records. After one dropdown bug, check every similar dropdown.
    • Secure and private by design. No secrets in code, least access by default, personal data minimised and protected. DPDP is the law now.
    • Think about scale. Will this still work with 50,000 records and 200 users? Bulk operations beat loops that call the server row by row.
    Root 6 · Plan the buildA mini product-management mindset: what first, what next.
    • Break it down. "The NGO wants a dashboard" becomes ten questions, your assumptions, the components, and a build order.
    • Order by dependency and value. Master data first, then core transactions, then reports, then automation, then AI. Technology has three jobs, in order: infrastructure, then acceleration, then insight.
    • Estimate before you promise. Use the scoping advisor skill. Plan the sprint before sprint one.
    • Every change has a price. Put an effort number on it and agree what moves.

      What social change looks like

      A seed bank keeps the seeds of the future safe. This one keeps ideas: 68 people, organisations and public systems that changed lives with technology, enterprise and stubbornness. Browse it when you need to remember what's possible.

      Patterns worth stealingWhat these stories have in common

      Read enough of them and the same moves keep showing up. Each one is a design principle you can use on your next project.

      Dignity, not charity

      Make people partners

      Goonj pays villages in goods for community work. Karya pays rural workers fairly for AI data, and shares resale earnings with them.

      Waste is a raw material

      Find value nobody saw

      Phool turns temple flowers into incense. Ashaya turns chip packets into sunglasses. Arvind Gupta turns trash into science toys.

      Meet people where they are

      Use what's already in hand

      Rocket Learning teaches through WhatsApp. M-Pesa built on people already passing airtime around. Gram Vaani works over an ordinary phone call.

      Process is innovation

      Design the system, not just the product

      Aravind borrowed assembly-line thinking for cataract surgery. The dabbawalas run on a colour code, with no computers at all.

      Build shared rails

      Open beats one-off

      UPI, Sunbird, AI4Bharat and Wikipedia are built once and used by everyone. It's the same idea as "stop rebuilding the same system".

      Ownership changes everything

      Let communities own it

      Amul's three million-plus milk producers own it. Every Lijjat sister is a co-owner. SEWA is a union of the women it serves.

      Measure what matters

      Evidence moves policy

      John Snow's map, Nightingale's rose diagram, ASER's reading survey and J-PAL's trials all changed decisions with data.

      Frugal by design

      Constraint is a feature

      A ₹65,000 pad machine. A clay fridge with no electricity. An infant warmer at a sliver of an incubator's cost.

      Field note

      Two of these seeds were planted very close to us: PadMan in Coimbatore and Aravind in Madurai. World-changing work doesn't need a big city. It needs someone who won't stop noticing.
