Founding thesis

Systems built for the
world's hardest work.

Six commitments behind everything EdZola builds, from a first field form to an AI worker.

I

Systems are still the foundation.

AI changed what's possible on top of a good system. It didn't change what a good system is. One record per person, from first visit to funder report. Forms that collect the same thing the same way every time. "Enrolled" meaning the same thing in every district. A dashboard the director actually opens. Most failed technology we inherit got the order wrong: analytics on top of five spreadsheets, automation on top of a process nobody wrote down. Automate a confused workflow and you get a confused workflow at scale. So we still start with the system of record. AI arrives after it, not instead of it.

Infrastructure, then acceleration, then insight. In that order. Read why →
II

Every project must compound.

A girls' education programme in Bengaluru and a literacy programme in Delhi ask the same questions about who they serve and what changed. Usually both pay to answer them from scratch, and the answer stays locked inside one organisation. We won't work that way. Every engagement has to leave something behind beyond the partner's own system: a tested template, a cleaner data model, a playbook, a decision written down so the next team doesn't relearn it. The partner gets a system built around them. The sector gets a little less to rebuild. That's how 60+ implementations became ImpactOS, and how one careful build becomes the starting point for the next ten.

We don't start from zero, so the next organisation doesn't either. Read why →
III

AI handles volume. People handle consequence.

We're AI-native, and we run our own work that way. AI is very good at volume: reading 5,000 scholarship applications, interviewing participants by phone in their own language, chasing missing documents, pulling themes from field notes nobody has time to read. It should not decide who gets the scholarship, whether a child is safe, or what a community said it needs. So every system we build draws that line on purpose. AI collects, checks, drafts and flags, with the evidence attached. A named person decides wherever the decision changes someone's life. Insight nobody is ready to act on is noise. A decision nobody is accountable for is worse.

IV

Judgement should become accessible.

The sector's problem was never access to tools. The tools exist. What's scarce is judgement: which question to ask before choosing software, what a beneficiary record actually needs, when a spreadsheet is enough, what will break in month five. Well-funded institutions buy that judgement by the day. Most nonprofits never get it at all. This is where AI genuinely changes our work. The way we decide, refined across six years of builds, can now be packaged into tools a programme manager can use at 11pm without booking a call. Preview the thinking freely. Pay when something becomes worth acting on. We still bring a person in for the hard calls. We just stop rationing the rest.

V

The people closest to communities deserve serious infrastructure.

The organisations nearest to the people they serve, a health team in a rural block, a grassroots livelihoods collective, a child protection unit with three staff, are usually the last to get good systems. They inherit the abandoned database and the spreadsheet with twelve tabs. That's backwards. The closer you are to a community, the more your data matters and the less room you have for error. Serious means offline forms in the local language, screens a field worker can read, access controls that protect the people in the records, a rollout scoped to the capacity that's actually there, and a team that stays through adoption. It's also why the Built for Impact Fund exists: to pay for systems these organisations can't afford, built and supported properly.

Serious social impact work deserves serious systems.
VI

We build openly where possible. Not everything is automatically open-source.

A free tool nobody knows how to run isn't free. So we decide what to open deliberately. Knowledge is open by default: field guides, frameworks, data models, sector maps and playbooks, free whether or not you ever work with us. Code is open where we can support it, with documentation and someone to call when it breaks. Some things stay closed on purpose: products like Zolabs and ZolaShield that fund the rest of the work and need someone accountable for them, and partner configurations that aren't ours to share. And data is never open. Beneficiary and donor data belongs to the partner and to the people in it. It isn't training material, and it isn't ours.

We learn one organisation at a time. We write it down so the next thousand don't have to start from zero. That is the direction. That is the work.
